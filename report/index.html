Society Feedback
📊
Proving program ROI to administration is the universal pressure point — and at some schools, it's tied directly to funding, cross-departmental buy-in, or closing a measurable career learning outcomes gap.
Across every school type — community colleges, technical colleges, private liberal arts, large public research universities — advisors cited outcomes data as their most urgent need. Most are operating without any reporting visibility: one advisor has manually tracked alumni on LinkedIn every year since 2015. Society's reporting layer isn't a feature; it's the strongest internal advocacy tool advisors have been missing. At Alvin College, the stakes are even higher: Texas outcome-based funding means the post-graduation tracking gap has direct budget implications, making Society's planned Handshake, LinkedIn, and Department of Education integrations a compliance and funding solution, not just a product nice-to-have. At Youngstown State University, Ohio's state-level mandate around workforce readiness and work-based learning creates a parallel institutional pressure point: Rebecca McCallister identified completion and career readiness data as the specific output her office needs to satisfy those mandates — and noted that expanding platform access beyond NSLS members to all 12,000 enrolled students is the condition under which institutional-level reporting would become truly valuable. At Southern Utah University, Brandon Street's T-Bird Ready program — built on eight competencies, evidence submission, and a badging system — has surfaced a precise data gap: a career readiness score dashboard that quantifies student progress against those competencies. Society's career readiness score and achievement engine concepts map directly onto this need, positioning SUU as a high-value co-development partner. At La Roche University, the reporting need extends beyond the advisor's own desk: peer-benchmarking data filtered by comparable school profiles (small, private, Catholic, suburban) is the specific currency that would unlock buy-in from the VP of Financial Aid, marketing, and admissions — all of whom already rely on peer-school comparisons as a standard decision-making lens. At the University at Albany, Jermaine Harris framed the gap as institutional rather than programmatic: higher education is “data rich but analytic poor,” where outcome data exists but is poorly cleaned, managed, or analyzed. He wants a student outcome “report card” and accurate first-destination data, and noted that self-reported capture is the only viable path because employers will not share hiring outcomes. Sari Khatib’s survey response sharpened this into four specific measures — jobs acquired, student satisfaction in role, employer evaluation of the student, and salary range — making UAlbany one of the few schools to name the exact ROI metrics leadership would need to see, rather than asking for reporting in the abstract.

The Clarity Track is reaching students that leadership programs never could.
Five schools independently flagged a student segment that won't engage a "leadership" frame: trades students, English majors without career direction, non-traditional learners, and commuters skeptical of the program's value. Each advisor saw the Clarity Track as the first credible bridge to these students. This is a significant untapped market, not a product edge case — and it came up without prompting in every school type. At Youngstown State, Rebecca McCallister identified the 'career exploration appointment' — where students arrive with little direction — as a recurring pain point, and saw Society's Clarity Track as a way to prepare those students before they walk in the door, a use case that extends the track's value directly into the advising workflow.

⚙️
SNT friction is a recurring product bottleneck at the advisor layer.
Five schools surfaced specific workflow problems around SNT group management: an advisor who can't facilitate matching because she's occupied with attendance scanning, students confused about group membership visibility, manual workarounds like color-coded paper systems, a large online university already building competing functionality internally, and Henry Stanford at Muskingum confirming the platform-wide 40–60% SNT drop-off pattern firsthand — with students skipping groups entirely or completing only one of three. SNT automation — scheduling, matching, in-app communication, and completion nudges — is the most consistent product gap mentioned by advisors who are otherwise enthusiastic.

🧩
Advisors are asking for consolidation before we pitch it — students are drowning in platforms.
At two schools the need was named unprompted, before the relevant feature was introduced — the strongest form this signal takes. Lauren Breckenridge at Texas A&M Corpus Christi described notification fatigue across iEngage, the NSLS platform, GroupMe and email, said students have begun tuning all of it out, and asked for “one designated spot for it all” before Society's communication hub was shown. Sari Khatib at the University at Albany cited direct student feedback asking for a one-stop shop for resources, and framed reducing platform overload as her primary motivation. Her colleague Jermaine Harris described the same fragmentation on the staff side of career services — StrengthsFinder, Focus 2, resume AI tools and mock interview platforms all siloed in separate systems — and made the competitive stakes explicit: everyone is looking for a one-stop shop, but the ones that exist are either poor quality or priced out of reach for state schools. The same instinct appears inside the SNT workflow at Madison Area Technical College, where Cole Kallio-Crotteau has no centralized meeting or messaging tool and coordinates by email, and at Muskingum University, where Henry Stanford called in-platform scheduling, matching and video rooms a “one-stop shop.” The strategic read: consolidation is not a feature request, it is the frame advisors already use to evaluate whether Society earns a place on campus. A platform that adds a tenth destination loses on arrival; one that absorbs several wins before its individual features are compared.

Paying members who don't complete is felt as an ethical failure — and advisors want the platform to share accountability.
Dan Balski at Arapahoe Community College named it plainly: over half of paying members don't complete the program, and this has not historically felt like a national-level priority. He framed Society's expanded benefit layers as a direct response — students who don't engage with the leadership track now have meaningful alternatives that justify the membership fee. Henry Stanford at Muskingum University confirmed the same pattern: SNT completion drop-off is the primary point where students disengage, with many skipping groups entirely or completing only one of three. Both advisors explicitly named platform-level nudges as the necessary intervention. This concern is not isolated to one school type — it spans community colleges and private universities alike.

#1discussed at 22 schools
Career Readiness Score
A Career Readiness Score — a composite metric reflecting student progress across skills, assessments, and career milestones — surfaced as the mechanism for bringing Career Services advisors under the NSLS tent. Where advisors currently struggle to demonstrate program ROI to institutional leadership, a visible score creates a shared language between NSLS and career services departments that today have no reason to log into the platform. Cassandra Gonzalez at Drew immediately connected it to how she thinks about student progress beyond induction completion. Natalie Maio at St. John's saw it as a tool that could support university rankings. Hunter Hammock at UTK confirmed it would help make the case to campus partners. Henry Stanford at Muskingum went further: he proposed that the score should include school-customizable components — internships, high-impact experiences, faculty research — because what counts as career readiness at Muskingum may not matter at another school. This is the most specific product-design input the idea has received, and came entirely unprompted. Robert Coates at Dartmouth, who has a background in measurement and evaluation, reacted positively to the score as a tool for donor reporting and demonstrating program ROI to institutional leadership. Heather Cordner at Keiser made the score the centerpiece of her decision to commit to the pilot, framing it as the metric her advising team needs to prove ROI across 5,000+ students. Nyeem McDaniels at Benedict — actively rebuilding career services ahead of a new department head — pointed to the score as the data infrastructure his college needs for marketing, college rankings, and faculty buy-in. Carl Hicks at Keystone tied the score directly to retention: Keystone serves a high proportion of first-generation and financially insecure students, and a visible career-readiness metric is how a top-10 U.S. News upward-mobility school defends its ranking. Elizabeth 'Bets' Murray at Northwest Vista raised the score unprompted as a way to bring NVC's career experience team and career navigators into Society — not just NSLS advisors. Morgan Sanchez at Palo Alto went a step further, asking that Society push the score and engagement data into the Alamo Experience platform so student participation appears on the unified district dashboard advisors already use. At Johnston, Megan Shaner connected the readiness score to her core frustration that career services only engages students post-graduation — a tangible career-readiness metric would bring NSLS into the career conversation while students are still on campus. At Alvin, where Texas's outcome-based funding model ties institutional dollars to where students land after graduation, Tonya Jefferson saw the score as exactly the kind of metric the college can show its funders. At La Roche, Paige Grogan connected the score to recruiting and admissions — she had already begun pitching NSLS at her VP level, and a quantified readiness number is what marketing needs to promote the program institutionally. At Southern Miss, Michael King wired the score into Dr. King's retention strategy — financial aid gaps are the primary driver of student attrition, and the score gives the office a quantitative way to identify which students are at risk before they leave. At Texas A&M, Patricia Alexander framed the score as the validation her Career Services team has been pushing for from inside the institution; her department is actively building a career success framework and the readiness number is the missing top-line metric. At Southern Utah, Brandon Street saw immediate alignment with SUU's 'T-Bird Ready' program — an eight-competency, evidence-submission, badging system that already maps onto Society's career architecture. The score becomes the way SUU reports T-Bird Ready outcomes to leadership and accreditors. At Youngstown State, Rebecca McCallister tied the score directly to her recurring 'career exploration' appointment pain point — the score gives her a defensible answer to students arriving without direction. At Columbia Southern (CSU) — an all-online university with ~19.6k NSLS members — Katelyn Garrison flagged career-readiness data as the platform's headline value: student-inputted job titles, dream jobs, and career direction history become the reporting layer their career services team currently lacks.

#3discussed at 17 schools
School Agent
The school agent concept comes up in most meetings: an AI agent trained on school-specific data — the course catalog, staff directory, student services, on-campus programs and resources — so the Society AI coach can give students contextually relevant guidance about their specific institution. Rather than generic career coaching, the school agent would know what TLU's directed studies look like, who the UTRGV career advisors are, and what resources NSLS members at St. John's can access after completing the program. The goal is to turn the AI coach from a general-purpose tool into one that can answer "what do I do next — at my school?" Cody Mazzurana at Texas Lutheran immediately envisioned it routing students to TLU faculty, career development staff, and internship opportunities. Hunter Hammock at UTK followed up with questions about integrating career services and academic advising into the coaching experience. Chris demoed the school-specific agent concept for Dartmouth — showing how the coach could reference Dartmouth's course catalog and the Center for Career Design specifically — and both Bob Coates and Travis Owusu reacted positively. At Keiser, Benedict, and Keystone the school-agent concept was a centerpiece of the demo — Heather Cordner, Nyeem McDaniels, and Carl Hicks all responded positively to agents trained on each campus's course catalog and staff directory. At CSN, Charlene Polite-Corley made the school agent an explicit condition: her excitement score would move from 8/10 to 9.5/10 the moment the AI coach can recommend CSN-specific courses, advisors, and on-campus resources rather than generic guidance. Johnston Community College, Alvin College, and La Roche University all reacted positively to the school-agent concept during the demo — each advisor (Megan Shaner, Tonya Jefferson, Paige Grogan) saw it as the bridge that closes the gap between generic AI coaching and the kind of school-specific direction their students actually ask for. Rebecca McCallister at Youngstown State responded particularly strongly to the school-agent concept, connecting it to her recurring 'career exploration' appointments where YSU-specific majors, advisors, and on-campus resources are exactly what students need surfaced. Katelyn Garrison at Columbia Southern responded strongly to the school-agent concept: as an all-online school, personalizing the coach to CSU's course catalog and career services is what closes the campus-connection gap. Tyrone McKenney at the CCU Graduate chapter also validated the school-agent path — the graduate chapter is greenfield and async-first, making it a natural test bed for the school-agent + AI-coach loop as the core curriculum surface.

#5discussed at 7 schools · raised unprompted
FOL Micro-Learning & Modular Content
The Foundations of Leadership curriculum in its current form requires advisors to manually time-stamp long videos and build their own facilitation guides from scratch. Kimberly Giorgio and Cassandra Gonzalez at Drew rebuilt the entire LTD facilitation guide themselves because the existing videos — designed for virtual/asynchronous delivery — don't translate to a live room. Dawn Vanniman at Mott refers to the current video instructor as "Karen" and calls the content scripted and inauthentic; her chapter uses it selectively and supplements with original programming, including Kahoot quizzes that consistently outperform the video for engagement. Melissa De La Cruz at TLU flagged that interactivity is what keeps student interest — the shift away from long-form passive video was immediately welcomed. Henry Stanford at Muskingum requested deeper leadership content specifically (more on emotional intelligence and leadership styles) and the session notes flagged FOL 2.0 with modular, interactive learning as the direct response. The common thread across all four schools: advisors are already doing the curriculum work themselves, building workarounds that the platform should provide natively. The proposal is short, self-contained clips and micro-learning units — concept cards, mini slideshows, embedded quizzes — that advisors can use independently or string together without any manual time-stamping required. At CSN, Jane reframed Society's Clarity track as a 'carrot' for completing Foundations of Leadership — implicit acknowledgment that the current FoL drop-off is solvable by modularization plus a reason to finish. At Northwest Vista, Elizabeth 'Bets' Murray connected modular FoL content to NVC's administrative pushback against a one-size-fits-all leadership program; modular delivery matches NVC's student-centered, student-led institutional mandate.

Personality Assessment Enrichment
Society's personality assessments currently present results without much scaffolding for interpretation — and some clinical framing carries unintended baggage (e.g., "high neuroticism" in Big Five). Dawn has built her own workaround: she and her co-advisor share their own DISC profiles with students, assign them nicknames ("the queen woo-hoo" for high-I), and ground the results in concrete, relatable scenarios ("if you want to just cry in someone's office, this is the office; if you want a matter-of-fact solution, come to mine"). Students who might tune out a clinical summary instead respond with "oh yeah, that makes sense." The extension: celebrity or public-figure profiles — mapping a student's results to a well-known person with a matching type creates an immediate, memorable hook. The deeper proposal is reworking the full assessment experience to be explanatory rather than clinical: show students what their profile means in practice, how it connects to the AI coach's guidance, and why self-knowledge makes the platform more useful to them. At the University at Albany the ask runs in the other direction: Jermaine Harris's team already uses Gallup StrengthsFinder as part of its regular practice, and he raised the prospect of seeing it surfaced alongside Society's built-in assessments rather than living on as a parallel tool students complete separately. That is an integration question rather than an interpretation one — recognising the instruments a campus already runs, so Society consolidates the student's self-knowledge instead of adding a competing profile to it.
