Section 1 — School Snapshot

School: Central Wyoming Community College (CWC)

Location: Riverton, Wyoming — a rural community approximately 20 miles from Lander, situated near the Wind River Range and in close proximity to the Wind River Indian Reservation.

School Type: Community college with a notable breadth of programming, including CTE (Career and Technical Education), a Bachelor of Applied Science (BAS) program with specialty tracks (Tribal Leadership, Organizational Management, Agricultural Management, Outdoor Education Management, Early Childhood Education), and workforce development partnerships.

Size/Culture: Small, rural institution serving a diverse student population that includes part-time commuter students, tribal community members, and working adults. Many students complete coursework primarily or entirely online.▶ 10:00 Student population described as transient, with many balancing outside obligations.

Chapter Status: Established

Chapter Type: Online (transitioned to fully online this academic year▶ 9:03)

Advisors Present: Lisa Appelhans (sole advisor present) — Student Services, chapter advisor

Notable Institutional Context: CWC recently revamped its mission statement and Institutional Learning Outcomes (ILOs), which now include civic engagement, personal and professional responsibility, and communication. The college has a CTE advisory council structure and active workforce development partnerships, including a Recompete Grant in collaboration with Wind River Development Fund. A grant-funded career services program is ending this year, creating an active need for sustainable career readiness infrastructure.▶ 15:48 State legislative priorities in Wyoming have elevated CTE, workforce training, and on-the-job certification programs. CWC is one of seven community colleges in Wyoming and is uniquely positioned on or adjacent to the Wind River Indian Reservation.

Section 2 — Discovery Findings

What They Want to Preserve (about NSLS)

  • The opportunity for students to develop and strengthen leadership skills and their own sense of what leadership means to them — described as one of the core values of NSLS membership.
  • Cross-program student connections — Lisa specifically valued students "getting to know students that they might not interact with otherwise because they're not in the same degree▶ 7:00 programs." This peer mixing was seen as a distinctive benefit of NSLS that is being eroded by the shift to online.
  • The belief that membership is worthwhile: "I truly believe that it's worth whatever they're going to put [into it]." Lisa sees the program as high-value when students engage fully.
  • The community-building energy that engaged members bring: "The students that are excited and have lots of energy, like they get other students wound up, but they also show up for things."
  • The full suite of NSLS components (speaker broadcasts, SNTs, leadership training) — Lisa expressed no desire to eliminate any element, only to improve the experience around them.

What Isn't Working (Pain Points & Frustrations)

  • Online transition has weakened peer connection: Since moving to a fully online chapter this year, Lisa noted that "things are so scattered" and there are "not as many face-to-face connections with students." The cross-program mixing that made NSLS distinctive is no longer happening organically.
  • SNT completion is not improving online: The move online was expected to make induction steps easier to complete, but completion rates have remained roughly the same. Students are not moving through steps faster despite the flexibility.
  • Scheduling mismatches persist: Even when Lisa offered both live Zoom and fully asynchronous options, students struggled to sync schedules — with each other and with her.
  • High drop-off after joining: A significant number of students pay the membership fee (~$95–$100) and then don't engage at all. Lisa expressed genuine puzzlement: "Why do you spend that and then not participate in anything?" She wants data to understand the dropout pattern.
  • Post-induction re-engagement is very difficult: Inducted members rarely return to mentor newer students. Lisa attributes some of this to the transient, part-time nature of the student body but sees it as a systemic gap.
  • Lack of meaningful reflection data: Speaker broadcast completions no longer generate qualitative feedback ("We used to ask students, what did you get out of this speaker broadcast…and we don't really see that as much anymore"). This limits her ability to assess learning outcomes.
  • Video content quality: Lisa did not raise this proactively, but aligned when Chris mentioned it — existing video content is seen as too long and not engaging enough for the current student population.
  • Generic community service vs. career-aligned volunteering: Current community service opportunities are driven by "what does the community need" rather than student career interests. Students in fields like social work or psychology don't get hands-on experience relevant to their goals.
  • Career services grant ending: The grant that has funded career services for 3–6 years is expiring this year, leaving a resource gap the school is actively trying to fill.

School Priorities (What the Institution Is Focused on Right Now)

  • Workforce readiness and CTE expansion: Highlighted at the state legislative level and embedded in CWC's institutional direction. Current active tracks include CNA certification, culinary certification, and trail building, with plans to expand.
  • Serving underemployed adults: The Recompete Grant targets adults ages 25–55 who are low or underemployed, connecting them to certification pathways that lead to better-paying work — particularly through the Wind River Development Fund partnership.
  • Tribal community engagement: CWC has a BAS specialty track in Tribal Leadership and collaborates with Wind River Development Fund. Serving tribal members is a meaningful institutional and community priority.
  • Demonstrating outcomes for accreditation and grants: While the institution no longer requires co-curricular assessment data for accreditation as it once did, Lisa is actively interested in data that could support grant applications and demonstrate workforce readiness outcomes.
  • Increasing student engagement and sense of belonging: Lisa wants students to feel connected to CWC as an institution, not just complete a checklist. Society was framed as something that could support belonging as well as career clarity.
  • CTE advisory council integration: Lisa has already initiated a conversation with the CTE dean about introducing NSLS to the CTE advisory council in the fall — a proactive institutional bridge she is building without prompting from NSLS.

Section 3 — Key Themes & Signals

1. Online Transition Has Broken the Social Engine of NSLS

CWC moved to a fully online chapter this year, and the outcome has been a measurable erosion of what Lisa identifies as NSLS's core value: connecting students across programs who wouldn't otherwise interact. SNT completion rates have not improved, peer energy is lower, and the distinctive community-building dynamic is fading. This is not a dissatisfaction with NSLS itself — Lisa remains a true believer — but a signal that the current online format is not recreating the experience that made NSLS work. Society's online SNT matching tool and integrated video conferencing were received as a direct and meaningful response to this problem.

2. Career Clarity Resonates as a Re-Engagement Hook for Disengaged Students

Lisa immediately identified a use case that mirrors the "Renee" story Chris shared: students who are close to graduating but uncertain about what comes next, and students early in their academic careers who need direction. She described Society's career clarity track as a potential on-ramp for students who would never engage with NSLS through the leadership door alone — including students who attend CWC purely for a credential and resist extracurricular participation. Her framing was that the platform could give students "usable skills" and ongoing support rather than feeling like "one more thing on campus."

3. Data Infrastructure Is a Strategic Institutional Need, Not Just a Nice-to-Have

With a career services grant expiring and workforce outcomes as a state legislative priority, CWC needs demonstrable data. Lisa responded positively to the career readiness score and administrator dashboard concepts — not just as tools for advisor convenience, but as instruments for grant writing, institutional reporting, and marketing. She specifically noted that being able to "not just tell the CWC story, but figure out why students are participating in NSLS" would help her know where to invest her energy and validate the program's impact.

4. Rural, Tribal, and Workforce-Focused Context Makes CWC a Distinctive Pilot Voice

CWC sits at the intersection of multiple underserved contexts: rural Wyoming, proximity to the Wind River Indian Reservation, CTE-heavy programming, and adult learners targeted by the Recompete Grant. Lisa has already initiated a conversation with the CTE dean about bringing NSLS into fall advisory council meetings. This proactive institutional bridge-building, combined with the unique demographic mix, makes CWC a high-value pilot partner whose feedback could shape Society's relevance for community colleges, tribal-serving institutions, and workforce-focused programs nationwide.

5. Student Recognition and Success Storytelling Are Underutilized Engagement Levers

Lisa identified a pattern: CWC recognizes NSLS members at big events (graduation, TRIO celebrations) but does very little to highlight student progress along the way. She expressed a desire to shift from "begging students to participate" toward celebrating what members are gaining. She connected this directly to the career readiness score and profile features — framing them as tools that could help students see their own progress and help the institution tell a more compelling, ongoing story of student success, both internally and for external marketing and grant purposes.
