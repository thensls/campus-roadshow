Section 1 — School Snapshot

Institution: Central Wyoming College (CWC)

Location: Riverton, WY — a rural community approximately 20 miles from Lander, situated near the Wind River Range and located on or adjacent to the Wind River Reservation.

School Type: Community College · Public 2-Year

Chapter Status: Established · transitioned to fully online chapter this academic year

Chapter Type: Online

Advisors Present: Lisa Appelhans (sole advisor identified)

Notable Context: CWC serves a rural, commuter-heavy, and part-time student population with strong ties to the Wind River Reservation and tribal communities. The college offers a broad CTE division (welding, cosmetology, culinary, automotive, medical assistant, entrepreneurship) as well as two Bachelor of Applied Science degrees with specialty tracks including Tribal Leadership, Organizational Management, Agricultural Management, Outdoor Education Management, and Early Childhood Education. A Recompete Grant supports on-the-job training and certifications targeting adults aged 25–55 who are low- or underemployed. Career services has been grant-funded for several years but that funding is ending this year. The chapter moved to a fully online model this academic year▶ 7:45; 30 new members joined this semester — more than recent post-COVID semesters▶ 9:19 — but induction completion rates have not improved▶ 9:28. CTE division advisory council meetings in the fall are being explored as a potential NSLS introduction opportunity▶ 1:06:41.

Section 2 — Advisor Profiles

Lisa Appelhans

Role: NSLS Chapter Advisor, Student Services — Central Wyoming College

Background: Long-tenured student services professional at CWC. Has personal ties to Utah (graduated from Judge Memorial High School; attended University of Utah). Interested in helping students build confidence to take career-oriented steps (e.g., job shadowing, reaching out to professionals) they might otherwise find intimidating. Communication Style: Candid, thoughtful, and reflective. Shares nuanced observations about her student population without prompting. Asks clarifying questions about timelines. Practical — flags real constraints (spring break, end-of-semester crunch, summer schedules) while remaining enthusiastic. Engages collaboratively rather than transactionally.

Pilot Partner / Product Council Potential: High. Verbally agreed to pilot partner program during the meeting. Expressed near-10/10 enthusiasm. Section 3 — Discovery Findings

What They Want to Preserve

  • The opportunity for students to try something different and strengthen leadership skills and self-perception around leadership goals▶ 6:20.
  • Cross-program peer interaction — students meeting and connecting with members they would not otherwise encounter because they are in different degree programs▶ 7:00.
  • The sense that NSLS is worth the membership fee for students who actively engage▶ 11:00: "They'll get out of participating whatever they decide to put into it."
  • The skills, interactions, and opportunities for students to showcase leadership on campus — seen as genuinely valuable even if not all member benefits are used.

What Isn't Working

  • The move to a fully online chapter has reduced face-to-face member interaction — a quality that prior members specifically said they valued most.
  • Online SNTs now allow students to form their own friend groups rather than being matched with unfamiliar peers▶ 7:58, reducing the "out of comfort zone" growth that defined the in-person experience.
  • Induction completion rates have not improved despite the shift to online — students are not moving through steps faster or more easily on their own schedules.
  • Scheduling conflicts remain a persistent barrier even when online and Zoom options are offered simultaneously▶ 9:59.
  • Students who pay the $95 membership fee frequently do not participate at all, and Lisa cannot determine why — she finds this both confusing and draining of her time and energy.
  • Post-induction re-engagement is very difficult; getting inducted members to return as mentors for newer students has not worked well▶ 55:51, which Lisa partly attributes to the transient nature of the student population.
  • Speaker broadcasts no longer prompt student reflection (e.g., "what did you get out of this?" questions are no longer surfaced), removing a source of qualitative data Lisa previously used for co-curricular assessment.
  • Career services grant funding is ending this year, creating a gap in institutional support for career readiness programming.

School Priorities

  • Workforce readiness and CTE outcomes were highlighted in the most recent state legislative session and are trickling down to all seven Wyoming community colleges.
  • On-the-job training and certifications (CNA, culinary, trail building) are being developed in partnership with Wind River Development Fund through a Recompete Grant targeting adults aged 25–55 who are low- or underemployed.
  • CWC's institutional learning outcomes (ILOs) were recently revised; NSLS is currently aligned to four of five ILOs: civic engagement, personal and professional responsibility, communication, and one additional (unnamed in the transcript). The fifth ILO — qualitative analysis — is not currently addressed by NSLS.
  • The college has two BAS degrees with specialty tracks including Tribal Leadership, intended to serve students who can complete entirely online.
  • Enrollment and student engagement are ongoing concerns; CWC has a large pool of students who meet NSLS eligibility criteria each semester but conversion to active members remains a challenge.
  • Lisa is exploring whether CTE advisory council meetings in the fall could serve as a venue for introducing NSLS more formally to that division.

Section 4 — Key Themes & Signals

1. Online Transition Has Eroded the Core Value Proposition

Lisa identified that the shift to a fully online chapter has weakened the peer-connection experience that members historically cited as their primary reason for valuing NSLS. SNT matching now defaults to existing friend groups rather than cross-program strangers, and face-to-face interactions across degree programs — which prior members explicitly praised — no longer occur organically. Induction completion rates have not improved to compensate. Society's online SNT matching tool and video-conferenced meetings were received as potentially restorative of this lost dynamic.

2. Career Readiness Is an Institutional Imperative — and a Gap NSLS Could Fill

Wyoming's state legislature has elevated workforce readiness as a priority. CWC's career services grant is ending. The Recompete Grant and Wind River Development Fund collaboration signal that practical, credentialed outcomes matter at both the institutional and grant-reporting level. Lisa responded directly to the career clarity track and career readiness score as tools that could help CWC tell a more powerful institutional story and potentially support future grant applications.

3. Students Need a Lower-Friction Path to Career Exploration

Lisa observed that students — particularly those in non-CTE programs like social work or psychology — lack hands-on career exposure. She described students as intimidated by direct outreach (e.g., walking into a department to ask about job shadowing) and suggested that a platform intermediary could reduce that friction. She also noted that students often have unrealistic salary expectations based on national averages without accounting for local cost-of-living realities. The career clarity demo, including its budgeting and location-based features, directly addressed this observation.

4. Engagement Drop-Off and Non-Participation Are Unresolved and Demoralizing

Lisa repeatedly returned to the puzzle of why students pay $95 and then disengage entirely. She wants data that would help her understand whether her outreach efforts are worth continuing and where to redirect her energy. She also flagged that post-induction mentorship re-engagement is nearly impossible with a transient, part-time commuter population. This is both a personal pain point and a structural challenge that Society's engagement tracking and career readiness score could begin to address.

5. Society as an Enrollment and Belonging Tool — Not Just an NSLS Add-On

Lisa unprompted raised the idea that Society could serve students who are attending CWC purely for a degree and a job — students who have no interest in extracurricular activities — as a way to build confidence, develop career skills, and feel more connected to the college. She also noted that CWC has a large pool of eligible students each semester and that Society could provide a compelling new reason to join and complete NSLS membership. Chris's suggestion that career services or academic classes could eventually feed students into the platform as a membership pipeline resonated with Lisa.

Section 5 — Society Reception

9/10

Lisa self-reported "almost a 10." Her only stated hesitation was timing relative to spring break, end of semester, and summer schedules — not skepticism about the platform itself.

What Landed Well

  • The career clarity track concept — giving students resources and a structured process rather than leaving them to "figure it out later."
  • The AI coach's role as a guide that prompts students to do their own research rather than doing it for them — Lisa appreciated the distinction explicitly.
  • The realistic financial and geographic context-setting (e.g., cost of living in a desired city, salary research) — she noted students often have unrealistic expectations based on national averages.
  • The career readiness score — valued both as an advisor tool for individual student meetings and as an institutional reporting and marketing asset.
  • The online SNT matching and video-conference meeting tool — seen as potentially restoring cross-program peer connection that was lost in the online transition.
  • The platform's potential as a lower-friction entry point for students intimidated by direct outreach to professionals or departments (e.g., asking to job shadow).
  • The idea that Society could serve non-NSLS-engaged students who are at CWC purely for a credential — framing it as a belonging and confidence tool, not just a leadership add-on.
  • The Renee story — a student who didn't complete FOL but engaged deeply with the career clarity track — resonated immediately. Lisa said she has students "just like Renee."

What Raised Questions or Friction

  • Timing: Spring break is the current week; classes end mid-May. Lisa expressed concern about the window available to pilot with students before summer.
  • Implicit question about capacity: Lisa asked about timelines before committing, signaling she needs a manageable rollout that works around her schedule and the academic calendar.
  • No explicit concern about AI, data privacy, or cost was raised — but these were not directly addressed in the meeting either.

Surprises

  • Lisa had already proactively spoken with CWC's Dean of Career Technical Education about introducing NSLS into the CTE division's fall advisory council meetings — an initiative she raised herself at the end of the call, without prompting.
  • She framed Society not just as an NSLS engagement tool but as a potential vehicle for students who actively resist extracurricular participation — expanding the addressable audience well beyond current members.
  • Her most pressing data need was not outcome reporting for accreditation (that requirement has been lifted) but rather understanding why students pay and don't participate — a behavioral insight question that no current NSLS tool answers.

Open Questions

  • How will mentor matching work in a rural context where local professional networks in fields like social work, psychology, or tribal leadership may be thin? Will the platform support connecting CWC students with mentors outside their geographic area?
  • Can speaker broadcast reflection prompts (qualitative "what did you get out of this?" questions) be restored in the new platform? Lisa previously used this data for co-curricular assessment and still sees value in it.
  • What does the CTE Dean's interest in NSLS at the fall advisory council meeting look like concretely, and is there a role for Society in that introduction?
