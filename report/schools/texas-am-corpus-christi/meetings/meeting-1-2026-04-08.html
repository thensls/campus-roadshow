Section 1 — School Snapshot

School: Texas A&M University-Corpus Christi (TAMUCC)

Location: Corpus Christi, TX

School Type: Public 4-Year University

Chapter Status: Established — runs cohorts each semester with new and returning members

Chapter Type: Hybrid — primarily in-person programming with a limited online track reserved for fully online students

CSR: Linda Condon

Advisors Present: Lauren Breckenridge (Activity Coordinator, Leadership & Traditions) and Jennifer Randolph (Director-level supervisor overseeing leadership umbrella including NSLS)

Notable Context: TAMUCC was named NSLS Chapter of the Year (2024). Their chapter is widely regarded as a model program — so much so that Texas A&M International reached out to adopt their operational approach. NSLS serves as the primary and foundational leadership program at TAMUCC; the university has built its broader leadership infrastructure around it. The chapter runs multiple in-person orientation sessions, leadership training days, speaker broadcast events, and SNTs per semester. A new AVP joined the university the day prior to this meeting, signaling incoming scrutiny around measurable goals and reporting. The chapter is also navigating active DEI restrictions under Texas state policy, which affects how certain Foundations of Leadership materials are delivered.

Section 3 — Discovery Findings

What They Want to Preserve

  • Lifetime membership access — cited as a key differentiator from Greek life, giving students ongoing access to leadership development without requiring organizational membership.▶ 8:01
  • The quality and integrity of NSLS content (Foundations of Leadership curriculum) — Jennifer noted that TAMUCC has built its entire leadership program around it and considers the content foundational, not supplemental.▶ 8:38
  • Optional advanced and executive tracks — Lauren personally completed these and values student choice in how deeply they engage.▶ 8:08
  • In-person programming model — multiple orientation sessions, leadership training days, speaker broadcasts, and SNTs are run live on campus each semester and are considered the chapter's core identity.
  • The "Grad Gear" incentive program — a homegrown engagement and reward system tied to community service, socials, and career development event attendance, which functions as a student motivation engine.
  • The SNT facilitator pipeline — facilitators must be inducted members, go through an application/interview process, complete training, and are limited to one or two SNT meetings per semester. This structure is considered a quality control mechanism.

What Isn't Working

  • Loss of the SNT facilitator portal — previously provided a structured, automated view of SNT weeks, facilitator assignments, rosters, contact details, and communication links in one place.▶ 9:00 Its removal forced Lauren to manually replicate the functionality via a custom Google Site and shifted significant administrative labor back to advisors and S&T co-coordinators.▶ 9:00
  • Dual email registration confusion — students register with both a school email and personal email, creating downstream problems: some students miss communications because they don't monitor one address; parents sometimes register students using the wrong email; students lose access to their school email post-graduation, threatening the "lifetime membership" benefit. Additionally, students who have already signed up continue to receive "you haven't signed up yet" reminder emails, causing confusion and prompting unnecessary advisor inquiries.
  • Communication fragmentation — the chapter currently uses iEngage, the NSLS platform, GroupMe, and direct email simultaneously, creating notification fatigue and disengagement among students. Lauren explicitly named "one designated spot" as a felt need.
  • Loss of pillar-based goal tracking — the chapter previously used NSLS pillars as internal benchmarks. With pillars removed, there is currently no structured replacement metric system, and the arrival of a new AVP has made this gap more urgent.
  • Manual SNT matching and roster distribution — advisors collect facilitator availability, manually build schedules, place students, export rosters, and communicate individually — a time-intensive process that Lauren has partially automated with a Google Site but which remains largely manual.
  • High advisor workload for induction follow-up — even with multi-channel communication, the team often resorts to personal texts and phone calls to push students across induction requirements, which is unsustainable at scale.

School Priorities

  • Induction rate — the chapter's primary KPI. Currently tracking 45–50%; the historical floor was 40%.▶ 16:20 With a new AVP starting, Jennifer expects increased pressure to demonstrate clear, documented goals and outcomes.
  • Showing program longevity and completion — not just new member enrollment but tracking members from prior semesters who have not yet been inducted and moving them through the program.
  • Career and workforce readiness — the university values career outcomes; TAMUCC's NSLS chapter supports this through its "Grad Gear" program, which awards credit for attending career development events (e.g., financial wellness sessions with the Dean of Students Office). There is an acknowledged gap in formal coordination with the Career and Development Center.
  • DEI compliance under Texas state law — the chapter has already removed certain Foundations of Leadership materials (e.g., the "iceberg" activity) from student packets to comply with state DEI restrictions. Jennifer explicitly flagged concern about AI-generated content potentially surfacing DEI-adjacent material in ways that could create institutional risk.▶ 1:01:00
  • Reporting upward — with a new AVP in place, producing clear, quantifiable metrics on student engagement and program impact has become a near-term institutional priority.

Section 4 — Key Themes & Signals

1. Operational Automation Is the Highest-Urgency Need

The single most concrete pain point in this meeting was the loss of the SNT Manager portal. Lauren rebuilt its functionality herself using a Google Site — a clear signal that the workflow had real value and that its absence created sustained friction. The manual processes around facilitator scheduling, student matching, roster distribution, and attendance tracking consume disproportionate advisor time. Any Society feature that automates or streamlines these workflows will have immediate, tangible impact at this school and should be treated as a priority design input.

2. Communication Consolidation Is a Student Engagement Lever

Lauren named notification fatigue directly — students receive messages across iEngage, NSLS platform, GroupMe, and email and have begun tuning them out.▶ 23:17 She anticipated that Society's centralized communication hub would address this before it was formally introduced, suggesting this is a genuine felt need rather than a product-prompted response. The centralized SNT chat feature in Society was received positively in this context. A single, authoritative channel could meaningfully improve engagement rates without requiring more advisor effort.

3. In-Person Programming Must Be Respected and Protected

Lauren raised an immediate and pointed concern when she saw the Foundations of Leadership online flow: students having self-directed access to steps they complete in person (orientation, LTD, speaker broadcasts) would undermine the chapter's in-person model and attendance. This is not a minor preference — it is a core part of how TAMUCC runs its award-winning chapter. The platform must be configurable to selectively enable or disable tracks and features based on each chapter's programming model. This chapter will not adopt Society as a full online replacement; they need it as a complement to, not a substitute for, their live programming.

4. Data Reportability Is Becoming an Institutional Imperative

With a new AVP starting and the removal of the pillar-based tracking system, TAMUCC is currently without a formal internal benchmark structure. Jennifer's interest in the advisor dashboard was explicitly tied to producing shareable metrics — for the new AVP, for the university, and potentially for external rankings (Jennifer responded positively when career readiness scores were mentioned in the context of U.S. News & World Report). Lauren added a specific use case: using data about students' resume completion status to trigger targeted outreach to campus career workshops. Both advisors are data-forward and would use this reporting capability actively.

5. DEI Compliance Is a Real Platform Risk in Texas

Jennifer raised the DEI concern without prompting and with specificity — she named the "iceberg activity" that has already been removed from student materials, and she expressed concern about AI-generated responses surfacing DEI-adjacent content in ways that could be screenshotted and escalated. This is not theoretical risk-aversion; it reflects active institutional compliance behavior. Society's AI guardrails and content moderation capabilities need to be explicitly communicated to Texas-based schools, and the ability for institutions to configure or restrict AI response parameters should be surfaced early in any pilot onboarding conversation.

Section 5 — Society Reception

7/10

Lauren self-reported: "Excited — 7, Intrigued — 10." Jennifer aligned with this framing. Both expressed genuine interest but noted they need time to process how Society fits into their existing operational model before committing more fully. The 7 reflects considered enthusiasm from a chapter that already runs a sophisticated, high-performing program and is evaluating fit carefully rather than reacting with uncritical excitement.

What Landed Well

  • The Clarity Track concept — both advisors responded positively to the idea of a career direction tool as a complement to Foundations of Leadership, particularly as a passive resource students can engage with on their own terms.
  • The Renee story — the example of a non-traditional student who didn't engage with leadership content but spent 70+ minutes in the Clarity Track resonated, reflecting their own experience with students who don't self-identify as leaders.
  • The advisor dashboard / career readiness score — Jennifer immediately identified this as useful for reporting metrics to new AVP leadership. Lauren connected it directly to an operational use case (targeted outreach to students who need resume help).
  • Centralized SNT communication — the idea of a built-in chat tool for SNT teams that advisors can also access was received as a direct answer to Lauren's stated communication fragmentation problem.
  • SNT matching automation concept — both advisors engaged substantively with this feature, offering detailed input about how their current facilitator scheduling and capping process works. The concept of automating that workflow was clearly appealing.
  • AI guardrails — Chris's explanation that the AI can be configured with content restrictions partially addressed Jennifer's DEI concern and was received with some relief.
  • Phased rollout approach — the suggestion to start with just the two advisors and the e-board, rather than an immediate full student deployment, reduced perceived commitment risk and enabled buy-in.

What Raised Questions or Friction

  • Online FOL access risk — Lauren's concern that students might bypass in-person sessions if FOL steps are available online was the most significant friction point of the meeting. She asked explicitly whether the platform could be configured to give access to some features (SNT matching, career tools) without enabling the full online Foundations flow.
  • Course recommendations (paid vs. free) — Jennifer asked whether courses surfaced by the AI coach would be free or paid and how students would be directed. This reflects both student financial concern and advisor accountability for what is being recommended to members.
  • Advisor data access and student privacy — Jennifer asked what advisors would be allowed to see. The permission-based model (students control their data visibility) was explained, but the concept of students opting out of advisor visibility is a potential operational gap for a chapter that relies on proactive advisor-student touchpoints.
  • Practical fit with existing workload — Jennifer was candid that she and Lauren are already stretched thin and need to evaluate whether this adds to or reduces their load before committing. The "how does this help with the extra 10 hours of work" framing reflects real capacity concern.
  • DEI compliance and AI-generated content — Jennifer's concern about AI responses surfacing content that could be screenshotted and escalated under Texas DEI restrictions was not fully resolved by the meeting's end. This needs dedicated follow-up in the pilot onboarding conversation.

Surprises

  • Lauren anticipated the centralized communication hub feature before it was formally introduced, naming "one designated spot for it all" unprompted — a strong signal that this is a genuine, pre-existing need rather than a product-prompted reaction.
  • The chapter has already built a workaround (Google Site) to replace the deprecated SNT Manager — demonstrating both the real value of that lost feature and the advisors' resourcefulness. Chris requested access to the site as a design reference, and Lauren agreed to share it.
  • Jennifer raised DEI compliance as a platform concern without any prompting, and with specific reference to a content element already removed from their FOL materials. This level of institutional compliance awareness was not anticipated and should be flagged as a consideration for all Texas-based school communications.
  • Both advisors framed Society's Clarity Track as a potential Grad Gear incentive mechanism on their own — recognizing it as a tool that fits naturally into their existing engagement reward structure without being prompted to do so.
