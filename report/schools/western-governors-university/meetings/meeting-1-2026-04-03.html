Section 1 — School Snapshot

School: Drew University — a small, close-knit liberal arts college located in Madison, New Jersey, near New York City. Known for its quirky, creative campus culture, strong theater program, and a reported ~70% student-athlete population.

School Type: Private liberal arts university. Small enrollment; advisors emphasized that Drew operates very differently from large institutions like Rutgers and that granular, relationship-level student data matters here in ways it might not at scale.

Chapter Status: New — launched this semester. Advisors described themselves as "literally just launched" and learning as they go.

Chapter Type: Standard (in-person, in-house). No hybrid or online component. Advisors handle all SNT facilitation, LTD delivery, and student tracking themselves without NSLS operational support.

Advisors Present: Cassandra (Casey) Gonzalez and Kimberly (Kim) Giorgio. Zoë Wallis (CSR) also present and provided context. Kim dropped from the call mid-session and rejoined partway through the demo.

Notable Context: Casey came from Lehigh University, where a Drew chapter was forming as she left. Kim drove the grant application and chapter launch; Casey joined and was included. Both describe themselves as highly organized and type-A. Kim has a corporate recruiting background (early talent, internships, co-ops) and has been at Drew nearly three years. The school's president is described as strongly innovation-focused, and Kim sits on multiple learning councils. Drew is actively building a new skills-based academic program that deprioritizes traditional declared majors. The Career Center and HR functions share an umbrella at Drew.

Section 3 — Discovery Findings

What They Want to Preserve

  • Leadership development as the core program focus — both advisors cited this as a primary reason they value NSLS.
  • Speaker broadcasts — both Casey and Kim explicitly called these out as a loved feature, including the ability to engage critically with speaker content.
  • The physical invitation process — Kim described the mailed letter as a meaningful differentiator that drove higher-than-expected student enrollment. She said students were genuinely surprised to receive physical mail and that it created excitement. She specifically flagged it as something NSLS "shouldn't lose."
  • The ability to track student progress through the system — Kim noted she appreciates having tracking mechanisms, even as she flagged current limitations.

What Isn't Working

  • Email/identity fragmentation: Students can register with their NSLS email, personal email, or school email. This creates reconciliation problems when advisors try to cross-reference with systems like Handshake. With 130 students, Kim and Casey had to manually build a separate list to match identities across platforms.
  • Lack of major/demographic data in NSLS platform: Advisors cannot pull reports by major, year, ethnicity, or engagement level. Kim described being unable to answer faculty questions like "how many marketing majors were inducted?" The platform does not surface this information.
  • SNT group management and visibility: Advisors cannot see which students are in which SNT groups from within the platform. They cannot confirm whether groups are actually meeting. Kim described having to maintain a separate external spreadsheet with group assignments. She said even when reviewing SNT reports, it was unclear whether the reported groups reflected real meetings.
  • LTD video format not suited for in-person delivery: Casey and Kim built their own facilitation guide because they found the existing LTD video content too passive for a live room. They described not wanting students to sit through an hour and a half of video content when they had gathered in person. They selectively pulled inspirational clips but recreated most of the facilitation content themselves.
  • Cost friction with students: Kim noted students push back on the membership fee given existing tuition burden. One student spent 30 minutes questioning why he was selected given his GPA, which prompted a coaching conversation about leadership not being GPA-dependent.

School Priorities

  • Career readiness and workforce preparation — Kim built employer relations and leadership development infrastructure from scratch and is focused on ensuring students can articulate their value and skills upon graduation.
  • Alumni engagement and professional networking — Kim identified these as areas she has been actively building since arriving.
  • Innovation at the institutional level — Drew's president is described as strongly focused on forward-facing initiatives. Kim sits on a council designing a new skills-based academic program that would eliminate traditional declared majors and integrate employer input into curriculum.
  • Demonstrating ROI on student programs — Both advisors want data that proves students are gaining something meaningful, not just checking participation boxes. Casey specifically cited wanting to know if students are "really getting something out of" clubs and organizations.
  • Skill development beyond goal-setting — Kim asked about expanding the program to include presentations, e-board development, and other concrete skills students can point to upon graduation.
  • Cross-functional engagement tracking — Casey described being regularly asked which students are "engaged" and struggling to define and measure that meaningfully across clubs, career events, and other touchpoints.

Section 4 — Key Themes & Signals

1. Data Fragmentation Is the Central Operational Pain Point

Kim and Casey are managing a 130-student chapter entirely in-house with no NSLS operational support. The inability to see SNT group assignments, student demographics, major breakdowns, or real meeting activity within the platform forces them to maintain parallel spreadsheets and manually reconcile identities across systems. This is not a minor inconvenience — it is an ongoing operational burden that consumes meaningful time for a two-person team running a brand-new chapter. The Society advisor dashboard, SNT tracking, and any future Handshake integration were directly responsive to this pain.

2. In-Person Facilitation Is Not Supported by Current Content Format

Drew runs a fully in-person, in-house chapter. The existing LTD video content was designed for virtual or asynchronous delivery and does not translate well to a live room. Casey and Kim built their own facilitation guide and selectively used clips rather than running the full video. This represents both a support gap and a product opportunity — advisors running standard in-person chapters need modular, facilitator-friendly content, not passive video streams. Casey offered to share their custom materials.

3. Career Clarity Is the Immediate Hook for Students Who Don't Engage with Leadership Framing

Casey's response to the Clarity track demo was immediate and emotional — she connected it directly to the students she sees most often: freshmen who "don't know what to do with their lives." The Renee story landed because it illustrated a student who didn't engage with leadership framing but spent 70 minutes in a career clarity experience. This school, with its liberal arts identity and students questioning the ROI of their education, represents an ideal test case for career-first entry into the platform.

4. AI Skepticism Is Real and Culturally Specific to This Campus

Casey flagged that a segment of Drew students is actively anti-AI — specifically around concerns about ethics, environmental impact, and inauthenticity, not job displacement. She described students writing a letter to the admissions team about an AI-generated Instagram caption. She noted that she personally uses AI in her work but communicates carefully about it, and that her office avoids announcing AI use publicly. This was not a soft concern — Casey explicitly said she thought about these students during the demo and that they would likely push back when logging into a Society experience. She offered to recruit these students specifically as test participants.

5. Meaningful SNT Participation — Not Just Compliance — Is the Goal

Kim described wanting SNTs to be substantive — specifically, she designed the LTD group assignments to pair students with people they don't already know, because working with unfamiliar people is itself a skill she wants students to build. She expressed frustration that the current platform makes it impossible to verify whether SNT meetings are actually happening or whether students are just logging report submissions. She framed this as a values issue, not just an administrative one: "We want it to be meaningful."

Section 5 — Society Reception

9/10

What Landed Well

  • The Clarity track — Casey responded immediately and personally, connecting it to students who frequently tell her they don't know what to do with their lives. She described it as "amazing" as a first step before coming to the Career Center.
  • The career statement / dream job output — Casey said she could envision students printing it and taping it to their mirror as a North Star. The specificity of job matching based on values, strengths, and environment resonated strongly.
  • The advisor dashboard — the prospect of filtering by cohort, major, and semester, and seeing student-level career readiness scores, directly addressed the data reporting pain Kim had described earlier. Casey acknowledged this would be useful even though she personally has more Handshake access.
  • SNT tracking within Society — when Chris described advisors being able to see group assignments, meeting status, and reports within the platform, this directly resolved the most operationally painful issue Kim had raised.
  • The AI coach framing as a partner, not a replacement — when Chris clarified that the goal is not for AI to take over but to hold students' hands through difficult self-reflection questions, Casey confirmed that came across clearly for her.
  • The phased pilot approach — Casey responded positively to the idea of advisors testing first, gathering feedback, then inviting small cohorts of students before general release.

What Raised Questions or Friction

  • Student adoption and activation: Casey's most direct question was "how do we get them to log in and start it?" She connected this to the existing challenge of students joining NSLS and not taking full advantage of what's available.
  • Profile privacy and data visibility: Casey asked whether student profiles would be visible to other students or to administrators. She was thinking specifically about whether she could use Society profile data to measure engagement quality across clubs and organizations beyond NSLS. Chris acknowledged this is not yet fully resolved and committed to FERPA-compliant controls.
  • AI skepticism among students: Casey raised this as a meaningful adoption risk specific to Drew's campus culture. She was not personally anti-AI but flagged that some students would resist or disengage immediately upon recognizing AI-driven features.
  • Human connection in next steps: Casey noted that after the AI coach interaction, the pathway to connecting with real people wasn't immediately clear to her. She said she couldn't fully articulate what was missing but felt that the post-clarity human connection step wasn't explicit.
  • Product Council time commitment: Casey asked directly about hours and start timing. Chris estimated approximately one hour per quarter for group meetings plus no more than ten additional hours per quarter for async feedback and individual calls, with a likely May start.

Surprises

  • Casey said she had "no idea" what to expect from the demo after completing the pre-meeting survey, and that what she saw was "definitely not" what she had imagined — framed as a strong positive surprise.
  • The degree to which Drew's campus has a specific and organized AI-skeptic student population was unexpected — not generalized discomfort, but students who had written a formal letter to the admissions office about AI ethics in marketing content.
  • Casey's offer to personally recruit anti-AI students to participate as Society test users was an unprompted and high-value signal of genuine partnership interest.
  • Kim's HireVue recognition ("I use HireVue — that's so funny") created an unexpected personal connection with the presenter, given that Chris co-founded the company, and reinforced Kim's corporate HR background as a strong framing point for the Society pitch.
