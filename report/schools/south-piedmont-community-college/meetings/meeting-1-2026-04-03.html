Section 1 — School Snapshot

School: South Piedmont Community College (SPCC)

Location: Monroe, North Carolina

School Type: Community College · Public 2-Year

Chapter Status: Established — Advisor has led the chapter since 2016 (~10 years)▶ 1:08

Chapter Type: Primarily in-person; orientation and some components offered online

Advisors Present: Connie Gardner (sole advisor on call)

Notable Context: SPCC serves a diverse student population including traditional-age students, older non-traditional students, a significant early college and dual-enrollment high school population, and students in skilled trades programs. The college is actively pushing to bring students back to campus post-COVID. A new president (less than one year in office as of the meeting) has reinforced a student-first institutional focus. The college is in a growth phase — new buildings are under construction (entrepreneurship and septic/trades), and an esports program is launching soon. SPCC competes with nearby community colleges for enrollment and has faced a community identity challenge (being mistaken for a satellite campus of a neighboring institution). The career area has been hosting job fairs open to community members. Connie also serves as Director of the Communication Center, a role she took on after years as a public speaking faculty member, and she has retained her NSLS advisory role through that transition out of personal commitment to student care.

Section 2 — Advisor Profiles

Connie Gardner

Role: Director, Communication Center; NSLS Chapter Advisor — South Piedmont Community College

Background: Connie began at SPCC as a public speaking faculty member and has since moved into the Director of Communication Center role, which is less than one year old as a physical space. She has advised the NSLS chapter since 2016 — approximately 10 years — and has deliberately not handed off the role because she wants to ensure the next advisor will care for students as deeply as she does▶ 1:13. She still teaches courses while holding the director title. She accompanied students to a national NSLS leadership summit in New Jersey (2022 or 2023) and has been a consistent internal advocate for NSLS, educating college leadership on the program's credibility and value.

Motivations: Connie is driven by student outcomes and authentic relationships. She values NSLS primarily for its ability to build leaders and help students develop interpersonal communication skills — something she sees as increasingly deficient among today's students. She is motivated by capturing more students who might not otherwise engage, including non-traditional students, trades-track students, and early college students. She wants NSLS to be credible and well-represented at the institutional level and to have data that demonstrates real impact. She shared a success story of a former chapter president who went on to earn a teaching position and a Teacher of the Year award, illustrating her focus on long-term alumni outcomes.

Communication Style: Warm, candid, and reflective. Connie speaks from direct classroom and advising experience. She raises practical concerns clearly and without hesitation (e.g., the orientation video confusion, the loss of the activity-tracking dashboard, the risk of Clarity being perceived as "less than" FOL). She asks clarifying questions and engages substantively with product details. She is not a pushover — she articulates nuanced feedback — but she is also enthusiastic and collaborative.

Pilot Partner / Product Council Potential: High. Connie expressed interest in both the Pilot Partner and Product Council programs when asked directly, saying "both of them, actually."▶ 0:14 She is an experienced, deeply engaged advisor with a long institutional history, a diverse student population, and a genuine desire to expand what NSLS can offer. CSR Dejeahn Brown describes her as "a prime candidate" for this feedback process. Follow-up meeting to discuss pilot rollout was agreed upon before the call ended.

Section 3 — Discovery Findings

What They Want to Preserve

  • The core mission of building leaders — Connie said explicitly: "Don't stop building leaders.▶ 6:47"
  • The induction ceremony — Connie asked directly whether it would still exist and was relieved to hear it would remain unchanged▶ 6:39.
  • The reputation and prestige of NSLS as an honors society — students at SPCC join specifically for the honor cords and the recognition at graduation; the program's credibility precedes itself on campus.
  • The in-person, community-building aspect of the program — Connie emphasized that NSLS helps students practice face-to-face communication, which she sees as a critical and eroding skill.
  • The SNT (Success Networking Team) model — she values SNTs as a structured way to get students meeting and working together▶ 6:39.

What Isn't Working

  • The orientation video conflates in-person and online chapter messaging▶ 9:13 — students who do orientation independently (without Connie present) receive mixed signals about deadlines and self-pacing▶ 9:57, leading to incomplete step completion. Connie noted the materials portal separates "live" and "online" versions, but the distinction is not clear enough in the video itself.
  • Loss of the activity-tracking dashboard — Connie noted that a previous dashboard allowed the chapter to track community service projects, fundraising, and other pillar-based activities. That functionality is gone, and she now can only see membership counts and step-completion status. This limits her ability to report chapter impact to institutional leadership and to show Executive Board members their individual contributions.
  • Advisor administrative burden — Connie described spending significant time sending repeated manual emails to students who have not completed steps, a task she described as time-consuming given that advising is on top of her full-time director role.
  • The old strategic plan requirement (already removed) — Connie mentioned this as something cumbersome that has since been eliminated; she was relieved it is gone.

School Priorities

  • Student retention and on-campus engagement — getting students back on campus post-COVID is a top institutional priority under the new president.
  • Student-first culture — the new president (less than one year in office) has centered the student experience as the primary institutional value, including listening sessions with students, faculty, and staff.
  • Expanding campus offerings to attract and retain students — new buildings (entrepreneurship, trades/septic), an incoming esports program, and community-facing job fairs are all active initiatives.
  • Community identity and visibility — SPCC is actively working to distinguish itself from a nearby college it is sometimes mistaken for, and to establish its presence and legitimacy in the surrounding community.
  • Career readiness across all student types — the college serves welders, trades students, dual-enrollment high schoolers, and transfer-track students alike; career readiness must be relevant across all of these populations, not just four-year-transfer-bound students.
  • Accessibility and accommodations awareness — Connie noted that some students in career-readiness tracks have varying intellectual abilities and need differentiated attention; she suggested the platform should ask about accommodations.

Section 4 — Key Themes & Signals

1. In-Person Connection Is a Core Value, Not Just a Preference

Connie repeatedly returned to the importance of face-to-face interaction. She teaches public speaking, directs a communication center, and views NSLS as one of the few programs that actively builds students' in-person interpersonal skills. She noted that since COVID, students have defaulted to online engagement and are struggling to communicate one-on-one or in small groups. Any platform feature that pulls students further into solo digital experiences — without reinforcing in-person touchpoints — risks undermining what she values most about the program.

2. Community College Student Diversity Demands Flexible Pathways

Connie made clear that SPCC's student body is not monolithic. It includes traditional-age students, students in their 40s, 50s, and 60s, early college high schoolers, trades-track students who plan to go directly to work, and students with special needs. She expressed strong enthusiasm for the Clarity Track specifically because it could serve students for whom the leadership track feels misaligned — welders, certificate-seekers, and career-changers. Her concern was that Clarity must be positioned as equally valuable, not as a lesser alternative to FOL.

3. Data and Evidence Are Critical for Internal Advocacy

Connie described a recurring challenge: educating institutional leadership about what NSLS actually is and demonstrating that her chapter is producing real outcomes. She previously relied on a pillar-based activity dashboard (now removed) to show engagement data to administrators and Executive Board members. She asked for that functionality to return in some form and specifically requested real student testimonials and outcome data — not celebrity endorsements or hypothetical scenarios — to make the case for Society internally and with skeptical stakeholders.

4. Advisor Capacity Is Stretched — Automation Would Be Genuinely Relieving

Connie is a full-time director who advises the chapter on top of her primary job. She described the manual effort of chasing students via email to complete steps as a significant burden. She responded positively when Chris described the AI coach sending accountability reminders on behalf of the system rather than requiring her to do it personally. Reducing administrative overhead — especially around student follow-up and SNT matching — was cited as a direct personal benefit.

5. Positioning of Clarity vs. FOL Will Determine Student and Advisor Buy-In

Connie raised a pointed concern: students at SPCC are motivated by the honor cord and the prestige of induction. If Clarity is presented as a separate track that does not lead to induction (as is currently the case), students may perceive it as a second-tier path. She stated this plainly: "I would make it important to be able to define clarity in a way that it doesn't seem less than." She acknowledged NSLS is working toward accrediting Clarity for induction purposes and supported that direction strongly.

Section 5 — Society Reception

8/10

What Landed Well

  • The Clarity Track resonated immediately — Connie said "that's awesome" when it was introduced and connected it directly to her non-traditional and trades-track students who may not self-identify as aspiring leaders.
  • Renee's story (the 68-year-old student who engaged deeply with the AI coach) was well received — Connie immediately mapped it to her own older members who are already established in life and looking to refine or redirect their careers.
  • The AI coach framing as "Connie in their pocket" — she responded positively to the idea of students having a guided, guardrailed assistant rather than open-ended AI, and to the idea of her having access to student data (with permission) to inform in-person conversations.
  • Personality assessments built into the platform — Connie noted these are typically expensive and that having them embedded would be genuinely valuable for students.
  • Automated student reminders — the idea that the system (not Connie) would send follow-up nudges was directly cited as something that would reduce her workload.
  • SNT matching tool — she expressed support for students being able to self-schedule and match into groups, especially in a hybrid or in-person context like end-of-Leadership-Training-Day matching.
  • Gradual rollout approach — she appeared reassured that Society would not abruptly replace the current system but would be introduced incrementally over two years.

What Raised Questions or Friction

  • Clarity Track and induction eligibility — Connie asked directly whether students who only complete Clarity would be inducted. The answer (currently no, but that is the goal) created a moderate concern. She anticipates students will ask this and wants a clear answer.
  • Clarity being perceived as "less than" FOL — this was her most substantive concern. She returned to it multiple times and framed it as a real risk given how honor-motivated her student population is.
  • The orientation video mixing in-person and online messaging — this is an existing friction point, not introduced by Society, but she raised it in the context of what is not working and what she would want fixed.

Surprises

  • Connie proactively raised the need for accessibility and accommodations awareness in the platform — she noted that some students in career-readiness tracks have varying intellectual abilities and that the system should ask about accommodations. This was an unprompted and specific product suggestion that Chris noted had not come up in this way before.
  • The loss of the pillar-based activity dashboard was a stronger pain point than expected — Connie described it as something she actively used to legitimize NSLS internally and to motivate Executive Board members, and its absence is a current gap she wants filled.
  • Connie expressed interest in both the Pilot Partner and Product Council programs without hesitation when asked — her enthusiasm for co-shaping the product was genuine and immediate.

A new dashboard with filtering and longitudinal views was described by Chris — Connie responded positively.
Student Profile / Career Statement Generation Positive Connie did not comment extensively but was engaged during the demo. The framing of a career statement as a student's "North Star" aligned with her values around personal direction and growth.
Advisor Access to Student Data (with Permission) Positive Connie responded well to the idea of being "superpowered" in in-person conversations by having access to what students have shared with the AI coach. Aligns with her preference for in-person engagement supported by digital tools.

Connie asked about this directly and anticipated students will ask the same. She supports the long-term goal of making Clarity induction-eligible but wants a clear, honest answer to give students now.

If Clarity appears to be a lesser path, it could deter uptake or create resentment. She said: "I would make it important to be able to define clarity in a way that it doesn't seem less than."
Orientation Video (In-Person vs. Online Messaging) Needs Care Existing friction point — not Society-specific. The current orientation video conflates messaging for in-person and online chapters. Students completing orientation independently receive confusing signals about deadlines and pacing. Connie always pre-explains this in person but cannot for students who do it remotely.
Short-Form FOL Content (FOL 2.0) Positive Connie did not comment extensively on this feature but was attentive during the demo. The idea of an advisor being able to show a short clip in-person and then direct students to complete steps on the app aligns with her hybrid chapter model.
Alumni Mentor Matching (2.2M Network) Positive Mentioned by Chris during the demo; Connie did not push back or raise concerns. The concept of connecting students to alumni in their industry of interest is consistent with her school's career-readiness priorities and job fair initiatives.
Accessibility / Accommodations Awareness in Platform Strong Unprompted suggestion from Connie. She noted that some students in career-readiness tracks have varying intellectual abilities and the platform should ask about accommodations. Chris confirmed this was a new and valuable signal and connected it to his own experience with a daughter on the autism spectrum.
Real Student Testimonials in Platform / Marketing Strong Connie explicitly said celebrity testimonials in the orientation video do not resonate with all students. She wants real peer success stories — students who started at a community college, went through NSLS, and achieved concrete outcomes. She shared a detailed example of a former chapter president who became a teacher of the year. She asked NSLS to find and feature more stories like this.

Open Questions
