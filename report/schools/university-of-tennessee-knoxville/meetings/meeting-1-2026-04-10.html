Section 1 — School Snapshot

School: University of Tennessee, Knoxville

Location: Knoxville, TN

School Type: Public 4-Year

Chapter Status: Established

Chapter Type: Hybrid (traditionally in-person; actively expanding to online/distance modalities)

CSR: Hunter Hammock

Advisors Present: Hunter Hammock (University of Tennessee, Knoxville)

Notable Context: UT Knoxville is a large flagship public university with approximately 40,000 undergraduate students. The chapter has historically operated as an in-person chapter and is navigating a significant transition as the university pursues growth in graduate enrollment and distance education programs — targeting an increase from ~7,000 to ~10,000 graduate students by 2030. Institutional priorities from the dean and president's office are centered on growing distance ed and graduate programs, and there is strong campus-wide momentum around career and workforce readiness. Hunter flagged the presence of well-resourced campus departments (career services, academic advising) with established programs that can be territorial, making cross-departmental collaboration a nuanced challenge. He also mentioned Susanna Marshman, Assistant Vice Chancellor for Student Life and Professional Development, as a key campus contact▶ 59:38 for potential collaboration.

Section 3 — Discovery Findings

What They Want to Preserve

  • In-person chapter experience and the networking and community that comes with physical presence — Hunter stated that multiple members have cited NSLS as their best campus experience specifically because of the people in the room▶ 21:21.
  • The sense of belonging that in-person engagement creates; Hunter linked this directly to what keeps members coming back and completing the program▶ 21:35.
  • The integrity and recognition of the NSLS brand, curriculum, and accreditation — Hunter wants innovation layered on top of what already works, not a replacement of it.
  • Flexibility to serve both traditional undergraduates who want the full in-person experience and distance learners or graduate students who need different modalities.

What Isn't Working

  • Engagement has dropped significantly since introducing online modality options▶ 6:24. Hunter attributes this to students choosing the path of least resistance (online) and then not following through — leading to confusion about where they are in the process and ultimately disengaging.
  • Students moving between modalities get confused about their progress, what steps remain, and what is required of them▶ 10:43. Hunter noted this is a communication and platform clarity issue that current comms and marketing have not fully solved.
  • The presence of a large, free, university-funded program covering similar ground (leadership, career modules) creates a competitive environment. Hunter noted 17,000 students "engaged" with it last semester, though he believes engagement is shallow (click-throughs, not completions).
  • Cross-departmental collaboration on campus is difficult due to territorial attitudes from well-funded departments like career services and academic advising.
  • No current mechanism in the platform to track or recognize member value beyond the chapter lifecycle — Hunter flagged that loyal alumni have no product to return to, representing revenue and engagement left unrealized.

School Priorities

  • Growing graduate student enrollment and distance education programs — the university is targeting an increase from approximately 7,000 to 10,000 graduate students by 2030, per Hunter's direct account of dean and president priorities.
  • Career and workforce readiness at scale — campus now requires all freshmen and sophomores to complete leadership, career services, and academic advising touchpoints before graduation, signaling deep institutional commitment to this area.
  • Serving non-traditional and distance learners with content tailored to their stage of life, not just traditional undergraduate programming.
  • Alumni engagement and lifelong membership — Hunter explicitly called out the opportunity to develop post-graduation products that leverage existing NSLS loyalty.
  • Demonstrating institutional value in a competitive environment where free, internally-funded alternatives are increasingly available to students.

Section 4 — Key Themes & Signals

1. Modality Confusion Is Driving Disengagement

Hunter identified a direct causal link between introducing online modality options and a significant dip in chapter engagement at UT Knoxville. Students are choosing online because it is convenient, then not completing, and losing track of their progress across modalities. This is both a platform UX problem and a student communication problem. Hunter's framing — "if you have a choice between in person and online, most people are going to pick online" — signals that the platform needs to do more work to guide students through the right path regardless of how they access it, and to make the stakes of each modality clear.

2. In-Person Belonging Is the Irreplaceable Differentiator

Hunter was emphatic that the real value of NSLS — what members themselves report as their best campus experience — is the networking and human connection that happens in person. He cited this in direct contrast to the university's own free digital program, which he believes students skim and abandon. This theme reinforces that Society must augment, not replace, the in-person layer, and that features like SNT matching, chat, and connections should serve to strengthen — not substitute for — live engagement.

3. Institutional Competitive Pressure Requires a Collaboration Narrative

UT Knoxville's campus is investing heavily in its own leadership and career readiness infrastructure, and well-resourced departments (career services, academic advising) can be territorial. Hunter's question about competitive differentiation was pointed and pragmatic. He also acknowledged campus ego dynamics directly. The signal here is that NSLS and Society need a clear, data-backed narrative for why they complement — rather than duplicate — existing campus resources, and that the admin dashboard and career readiness score could be powerful tools for making that case to skeptical campus partners.

4. Graduate Students and Distance Learners Represent an Underserved and Growing Segment

Hunter explicitly connected UT's institutional growth goals (7,000 to 10,000 graduate students by 2030) to a product opportunity. He noted that graduate and professional students are in a different season of life than traditional undergraduates and need tailored content. This aligns with his interest in lifelong membership and alumni products. Society's ability to serve non-traditional students — including through the Clarity track and a flexible modality experience — is directly relevant to UT's near-term institutional priorities.

5. Alumni Loyalty Is an Untapped Revenue and Engagement Opportunity

Hunter raised unprompted the idea of developing products for NSLS alumni who have graduated and remain loyal to the brand. He framed this as money being left on the table — suggesting that if NSLS offered continuing education, professional development, or mentorship products for alumni, loyal members would purchase them. He also connected this to the mentorship and connections features in Society, noting the value of alumni coming back to mentor the next generation. This is a strategic signal beyond the current platform scope that warrants tracking for future roadmap consideration.

Section 5 — Society Reception

10/10

What Landed Well

  • Hunter gave Society a 10/10 enthusiasm score, describing it as a "great sales pitch" and expressing genuine excitement to learn more.
  • The Connections track and the concept of matching students for SNTs algorithmically resonated — Hunter has long emphasized the primacy of in-person networking, and the idea of technology facilitating better matches (by major, affinity, personality) clearly aligned with his values.
  • The admin dashboard with cross-year data filtering and the career readiness score landed as directly useful tools — particularly for making the case to other campus departments about the value NSLS provides.
  • The vision of Society as a lifelong platform — carrying students from undergraduate through graduate and beyond — directly mapped to Hunter's stated interest in alumni engagement and post-graduation products.
  • Chris's framing of Society as a "force multiplier" for advisors, not a replacement for their work, appeared to resonate given Hunter's comments about wanting tools that help serve the volume and variety of students at a large flagship institution.
  • The school-specific AI agent concept — trained on the institution's course catalog, academic calendar, and staff directory — was presented as a future roadmap item, and Hunter's follow-up questions about collaboration with career services and academic advising suggest he immediately saw the practical application of this at UT.

What Raised Questions or Friction

  • Hunter's central question was about competitive positioning: how does Society differentiate from free, university-funded programs that already cover leadership and career readiness at scale? He did not frame this as a dealbreaker but as a real obstacle that needs a clear answer — particularly when approaching territorial campus departments.
  • He flagged that some campus partners (career services, academic advising) have strong institutional identities and may resist collaboration, noting "pretty big egos." This is a relationship and change management challenge that goes beyond the product itself.
  • The complexity of managing students across multiple modalities — and ensuring platform clarity as students move between them — was raised as an unresolved pain point that Society will need to address directly.

Surprises

  • Hunter's proactive and unprompted pitch for an alumni/lifelong membership product was the most forward-looking contribution in the session. He went beyond reacting to what was shown and actively proposed a new product direction, signaling a high level of strategic investment in NSLS's long-term trajectory.
  • The candor about campus political dynamics — specifically naming department egos and a specific campus contact (Susanna Marshman) — was notably direct and actionable for a group session. Hunter appeared comfortable advocating for NSLS's position even in complex institutional politics.
  • Hunter's observation that UT's own free program had 17,000 student "engagements" last semester, followed immediately by skepticism about the depth of that engagement, was a sharp and unprompted competitive analysis that reinforced NSLS's differentiation on quality and completion over volume.
