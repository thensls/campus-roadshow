Section 3 — Discovery Findings

What They Want to Preserve

  • The Broadcast Speaker Series — Sari cited this as a resource students value and that should not be lost in any platform transition.
  • The NSLS job board — identified by Sari as a meaningful member benefit students actively use.
  • Scholarship opportunities — Sari noted these as a third core value-driver for NSLS members at Albany.
  • Human-centered advising conversations — Jermaine emphasized that AI should prepare students for more efficient in-person meetings, not replace those interactions.
  • Institutional data integrity — Jermaine stressed the importance of accurate, well-maintained platform data and called out the risks of uncleaned records.

What Isn't Working

  • Platform overload — Sari reported direct student feedback about having too many places to go for information; students are asking for a consolidated resource hub▶ 4:56.
  • Back-end neglect — Jermaine discovered the NSLS platform had not been properly maintained at Albany, with data that appeared inflated or uncleaned over five-plus years▶ 48:14. He expressed frustration that no one had been managing or scrubbing it.
  • Disconnected tools in career services — Jermaine described the current landscape as fragmented: StrengthsFinder, Focus 2, resume AI tools, and mock interview platforms are all siloed across separate systems. Everyone is looking for a one-stop shop but the ones that exist are either poor quality or prohibitively expensive for state schools.
  • Student misinterpretation of interest-based tools — Jermaine flagged a recurring problem with Focus 2: students conflate interest results with capability or job prescriptions, leading to confusion that requires a follow-up meeting with a career coach to untangle.
  • AI misuse for generic outputs — Jermaine shared a specific example of two accounting students submitting identical ChatGPT-generated resumes without personalization▶ 15:54, illustrating a structural problem with unguided AI use.
  • Platform adoption and sustainability — Jermaine described a pattern of platforms being adopted and abandoned when staff leave or buy-in erodes, calling higher ed institutions "graveyards for platforms."▶ 57:33
  • University email expiration — Jermaine flagged that institutional emails typically deactivate three to six months post-graduation▶ 56:45, creating continuity problems for platforms tied to those credentials.

School Priorities

  • UAlbany's newly launched Thrive initiative establishes four institutional student success pillars: health and well-being, financial literacy, belonging on campus, and career and professional development.
  • Capturing and reporting on student experiential learning data (internships, outcomes) in a reliable, self-reported format — Jermaine cited the shift toward platforms like Handshake and 12twenty to enable real-time student self-reporting in response to employer data-sharing limitations▶ 3:36.
  • Increasing student engagement with career services and academic advising through more efficient, informed touchpoints — both advisors expressed interest in a model where AI prepares students for more productive in-person meetings.
  • Cross-divisional coordination — Sari emphasized that any platform affecting multiple units (engagement, career services, academic advising) requires broad staff buy-in and careful rollout planning across divisions.
  • First-destination outcomes data — Jermaine described this as a long-standing institutional need, noting that scrubbing LinkedIn data is already common practice among platforms serving this function.

Section 4 — Key Themes & Signals

1. Platform Consolidation Is a Student-Stated and Staff-Felt Need

Sari directly cited student feedback calling for a single place to access resources. Jermaine echoed this from the staff side, noting he has sat through demos of one-stop-shop platforms — some good, some too expensive for a state school. Both advisors framed fragmentation as a core problem. Society's multi-track, AI-unified architecture maps directly onto this pain point, but the promise has been made before and not delivered. Credibility will require demonstrating back-end simplicity, not just front-end breadth.

2. Back-End Usability and Staff Buy-In Are Make-or-Break Conditions

Both advisors converged independently on this theme. Sari raised it as her primary concern when rating Society a 7/10 — "the behind the scenes and what that's going to look like."▶ 49:13 Jermaine articulated the systemic version: higher ed is a graveyard for platforms when staff don't use them or someone leaves. His observation that the existing NSLS back-end had five-plus years of uncleaned data at Albany is a direct signal that ease of administration and ongoing support must be core design priorities, not afterthoughts.

3. AI Should Scaffold Thinking, Not Generate Answers

Jermaine articulated a clear and strongly held philosophy: AI tools are valuable when they guide students through a process, not when they hand students outputs to copy-paste. He shared a concrete example of two accounting students submitting identical ChatGPT resumes. He explicitly praised Society's coaching approach — "I love those platforms that help walk them through it instead of just giving them the answer" — making this a confirmed value alignment. This framing should be reinforced in all career services messaging for Society.

4. Career Services Integration Requires a Dedicated Pathway

Jermaine made clear that career services operates with its own tools, conferences (NACE, Eastern NACE), and internal structures — distinct from student affairs. He noted career services can sit under either student affairs or academic affairs depending on the institution, which affects how and to whom Society should be introduced. He sees value in the AI-to-human handoff model but will need to bring in his career education colleagues before any formal partnership. He committed to sharing the Society recording with that team, which is a meaningful voluntary next step.

5. Data Integrity and Longitudinal Outcomes Reporting Are Institutional Imperatives

Jermaine described higher ed as "data rich but analytic poor" — a systemic condition where data exists but is poorly cleaned, managed, or analyzed. He wants student outcome data (a "report card" of what helped students succeed) and first-destination outcomes that are accurate and self-reported. His awareness of LinkedIn scraping as a common practice signals sophistication. The proposed career readiness score and longitudinal advisor dashboard landed as directionally right, but the advisors will judge Society on whether the data it produces is actually trustworthy and actionable — not just voluminous.

Section 5 — Society Reception

7/10

Both Sari Khatib and Jermaine Harris independently rated Society a 7 (with Jermaine offering 7–8). Scores reflect genuine interest and alignment tempered by back-end concerns and appropriate skepticism from two experienced platform evaluators.

What Landed Well

  • The AI coach's guided, Socratic approach — Jermaine explicitly endorsed the model of walking students through thinking rather than generating answers for them.
  • The career readiness score concept — the idea of a pre-meeting snapshot that lets advisors understand where a student stands before they walk in resonated with Jermaine's vision for more efficient advising conversations.
  • The human-AI handoff model — Jermaine confirmed he has seen other products taking this approach and acknowledged it as the right direction, particularly for career services contexts with high student volume and limited staff.
  • The Renee story — the 68-year-old nontraditional student narrative illustrated that Society can capture students that traditional leadership framing misses, and that deep personalization drives engagement even in unexpected demographics.
  • The multi-email / login flexibility — Jermaine immediately flagged the problem of institutional email expiration post-graduation and was reassured by the ability to link personal and school emails within a single record.
  • NSLS's piloting strategy — Jermaine validated using the NSLS membership cohort as a built-in focus group before broader rollout, comparing it to his own practice of piloting tools with EOP counselors or specific courses.
  • Alignment with UAlbany's Thrive initiative — Sari recognized Society's four tracks as directionally consistent with the school's four student success pillars.

What Raised Questions or Friction

  • Back-end complexity and administrator burden — Sari's primary concern was the investment required to build and manage the platform institutionally, especially given that staff are already pressed for time and portfolios are shifting.
  • Interests vs. capabilities distinction — Jermaine raised a well-formed concern about whether Society's career recommendations reflect interests or capabilities, drawing on his experience with Focus 2 and the confusion it creates for students. He was satisfied with Chris's clarification but flagged this as an ongoing risk to manage in student communications.
  • Gallup StrengthsFinder absence — Jermaine noted the values exercise reminded him of StrengthsFinder and asked directly about integration. He confirmed the career education team at Albany is "heavily on" Gallup Strengths, making this a meaningful gap for that audience.
  • Career center integration pathway — Jermaine indicated Society is not yet ready for a formal career services partnership and that the right next step is looping in career education colleagues. He framed this as a sequencing issue, not a rejection.
  • Platform sustainability track record — Jermaine's observation about higher ed platform graveyards was a direct challenge to NSLS to demonstrate ongoing support, maintenance, and adoption infrastructure — not just a strong product demo.
  • State school budget constraints — Jermaine noted unprompted that some one-stop-shop platforms he has evaluated are "overly expensive" and impossible to get past purchasing at a state school, signaling that pricing will be a critical factor if Society expands beyond the NSLS membership bundle.

Surprises

  • Jermaine had zero prior NSLS familiarity — he encountered the organization only recently at NASPA and had not been engaged by the chapter at Albany despite working on the same campus. This represents a significant internal visibility gap for NSLS at the institution.
  • The existing NSLS back-end data at Albany appeared severely neglected — Jermaine discovered what he described as implausible user numbers (72,000) and called out a lack of data maintenance going back years. This was not raised as a criticism of Society specifically, but it reflects a broader trust and credibility challenge that the new platform will need to overcome.
  • Jermaine volunteered — unprompted — to share the Society recording with the career education team. This was the strongest organic signal of advocacy in the meeting and suggests he sees genuine potential for cross-departmental interest despite his stated skepticism.
  • Sari's question about human-coach complementarity surfaced an unmet need: the advisors want to understand how Society positions human staff in relation to the AI coach — a question about role clarity that the product team should address more explicitly in future demos.
