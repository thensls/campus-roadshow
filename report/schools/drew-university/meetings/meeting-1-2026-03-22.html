Section 3 — Discovery Findings

What They Want to Preserve

  • Leadership development as a core program focus — both advisors cited this as a shared passion and a foundational value of NSLS they want maintained.▶ 5:24
  • Speaker broadcasts — both Kim and Cassandra independently called these out as a feature they love and want kept.▶ 6:03
  • The physical invitation letter — Kim emphasized the mailed envelope drove unexpectedly high student engagement▶ 7:05 ("kids now don't get mail▶ 7:05") and cautioned against losing that touch even though it's cost-prohibitive.
  • The inspirational video clips — advisors want to keep short, motivational video content; they object to the format of watching long videos passively, not to the content itself.

What Isn't Working

  • SNT management and visibility — the current platform does not show who is in which SNT group, whether groups have actually met, or allow cohort-based tracking. Advisors are maintaining a separate spreadsheet with group numbers to compensate.
  • Student data and reporting — the platform does not surface major, year, ethnicity, or engagement breakdown. When faculty ask how many marketing majors were inducted, there is no way to answer that from the NSLS system. Advisors are building workarounds manually.
  • Email/identity fragmentation — students can register with any email (NSLS, personal, or school), which creates reconciliation issues when trying to cross-reference with Handshake or send targeted communications. With 130 students, they had to manually locate Drew emails for students who didn't register with one.
  • Communication effectiveness — emails sent from NSLS are less likely to get student responses than emails sent directly from Kim or Cassandra. The platform's communication tools don't match the relationship dynamics of a small school.
  • Passive video format for in-person LTD — the existing videos were designed for virtual/asynchronous delivery and are not suited for in-person facilitation. Advisors rebuilt the entire facilitation guide from scratch and used only short clips from the existing content.
  • Induction pricing — students questioned the additional cost on top of tuition. Kim noted she cannot subsidize all students and acknowledged this as an ongoing friction point.

School Priorities

  • Career readiness and workforce preparation — Kim built out employer relations and leadership infrastructure from zero. Career outcomes are the primary lens through which she evaluates programs.
  • Skill development beyond goal-setting — advisors want students to develop communication, collaboration, and interpersonal skills, not just SMART goal frameworks. Kim asked: "What other skills can they learn — could you put a presentation into it?"
  • Cross-group collaboration — both advisors intentionally structured SNT assignments to prevent friend groups from clustering, because working with new people is itself a transferable skill.
  • Innovation alignment with institutional direction — Drew's president is focused on forward-facing innovation, and Kim is on multiple learning councils. She explicitly asked how NSLS can bring innovative, forward-thinking opportunities to campus.
  • Engagement data with depth — Cassandra needs data that shows whether students are genuinely getting value from clubs and programs, not just attendance counts. She described this as a gap shared by most professionals in her area.
  • Skill-based education model — Drew is piloting a new school model where students earn degrees through skills rather than designated majors, with employers embedded in the curriculum.

Section 4 — Key Themes & Signals

1. Standard Chapter Operational Strain

Drew is running a fully in-house chapter with no SNT facilitation support from NSLS. Kim and Cassandra — both self-described Type A — are compensating with custom spreadsheets, redesigned facilitation guides, and manual email reconciliation across 130 students. Kim noted that someone less organized "would die" in the current system. This chapter represents a critical use case for small schools running everything in-house, and their pain points are disproportionately sharp because of it.

2. SNT Infrastructure Is a Core Operational Failure Point

The inability to see SNT group membership, meeting status, or attendance reports directly in the platform was the most detailed and urgent pain point raised. Advisors cannot verify whether groups are actually meeting, have no cohort-based view, and must maintain parallel tracking outside the system. This is creating real risk of the SNT process becoming meaningless — Kim said, "We can just say forget it, but we want it to be meaningful."

3. Career Clarity as Student Activation Entry Point

Cassandra responded with immediate enthusiasm to the clarity track, connecting it directly to her daily conversations with students who say "I don't know what to do with my life." She framed it as a powerful first step before students come to the Career Center. The Renee story resonated — the idea that a student who didn't engage with leadership framing might engage immediately with career framing was not abstract to her; it reflects her lived experience on campus.

4. AI Skepticism Among the Student Body Is a Real Adoption Risk

Cassandra raised unprompted that Drew students — particularly those in creative and socially conscious communities — are actively skeptical of AI on ethical and environmental grounds. Students wrote a formal letter to Drew's admissions team objecting to AI-generated Instagram captions. Cassandra acknowledged her own office avoids publicly disclosing AI use. She offered to recruit anti-AI students as pilot testers, framing Drew's student body as a meaningful test case for how to position AI-assisted tools with resistant audiences.

5. Data Depth Is a Strategic Need, Not Just a Convenience

Both advisors articulated a need for engagement data that goes beyond participation counts — major breakdowns, cohort tracking, evidence of actual skill development, and ROI for students paying the membership fee. Kim noted she cannot answer faculty questions like "how many marketing majors were inducted" from the NSLS platform. Cassandra described wanting data to show whether students are genuinely benefiting from clubs and programs — a gap she believes is widespread across student affairs professionals. A Handshake integration was flagged as potentially high-value.

Section 5 — Society Reception

9/10

Scored by Cassandra Gonzalez. She explained she is "never a 10 out of 10 girl" and held back one point specifically because she was thinking about AI-skeptical students who might not share her open-mindedness — not because of any deficit in the product itself.

What Landed Well

  • The clarity track concept resonated immediately — Cassandra connected it to her daily conversations with undecided students and called it "amazing" as a first step before students come to the Career Center.
  • The SNT management features shown in the demo directly addressed the biggest operational pain point the advisors had just described. Chris's preview of group visibility, meeting tracking, and in-platform SNT reporting was well-received.
  • The advisor dashboard with filtering by cohort, major, and semester spoke directly to the data gaps Kim had raised. Cassandra confirmed it would be useful, particularly if Handshake data could be surfaced alongside it.
  • The career statement output — Cassandra said she could picture students printing it and taping it to their mirror as a North Star.
  • The gamification elements (streaks, confetti, accomplishment tracking) were acknowledged positively in context.
  • The pilot rollout model (advisors first, then small cohorts, then general release) made sense and felt manageable.
  • Cassandra's overall reaction: "This is not what I was thinking about. I love this."

What Raised Questions or Friction

  • AI visibility and framing — Cassandra raised a direct concern that some Drew students will see "AI" and disengage immediately, citing the Instagram letter incident. She noted her own office deliberately avoids publicizing its AI use. The question of how to position an AI-forward product to AI-skeptical students is unresolved.
  • Profile privacy — Cassandra asked whether student profiles would be visible to other students or to administrators/employers, and whether that data could be used to assess engagement across clubs broadly. The answer was "not fully nailed down yet," with FERPA noted as a guiding constraint.
  • Student activation — Cassandra's overarching concern was adoption: "How do we get them to log in and start it?" She acknowledged Society is only valuable for students who actually use it, and noted that low-utilization is already a pattern with NSLS broadly.
  • Human connection in next steps — Cassandra noted the platform felt "very AI heavy" and wondered whether the pathway after clarity would connect students to real people, not just more AI interactions. She couldn't fully articulate it in the moment but flagged it as something she'd follow up on.

Surprises

  • Cassandra had filled out a pre-meeting survey and said she had "no idea" what would be shown — and that Society was "definitely not" what she had imagined. Her reaction was genuinely surprised and enthusiastic.
  • The depth of the AI skepticism at Drew was unexpected and specific — it wasn't general tech anxiety but a values-driven concern about ethics and environmental impact, articulated by students who took formal action against the university over it.
  • Cassandra volunteered, without prompting, to personally recruit anti-AI students to serve as pilot testers — framing it as a way to get perspective from the hardest-to-convert segment.
  • Kim recognized Chris as a HireVue co-founder, creating an immediate credibility moment around the career and HR technology connection.

Open Questions

  • How will student profile visibility be controlled — what can other students see, what can advisors see, and what can employers or external parties access? FERPA compliance framework needed before this is defined.
  • How should Society be positioned to AI-skeptical students who may disengage at the mention of AI? What language, framing, or UX decisions will help bridge this gap — especially at campuses like Drew where the resistance is organized and values-driven?
  • After the clarity track, how does Society connect students to real people — advisors, mentors, alumni — rather than continuing to route them back to the AI coach? This transition was flagged as potentially missing or underdeveloped.
  • What is the confirmed timeline for general Society release (noted as "most likely this fall") and what are the specific milestones between now and then that pilot partners should expect?
  • What are the exact time commitments for the Product Council — Cassandra asked specifically about hours per quarter and start date (noted as May, ~10 hours/quarter + 1 hour meeting). Kim has not yet reviewed or responded to this ask.
  • Can Society's advisor dashboard surface engagement data across campus broadly (clubs, events, admissions touchpoints) — or is it scoped only to NSLS activity? Cassandra's use case suggests broader cross-campus engagement data is the real need.
